Lecture 3.1 · The Hook

One Tap on the Coffee Cart App…

A student preorders a latte. For that single tap to work, the app has to remember:

Who ordered

Name, account, saved payment method, loyalty punches so far

What they ordered

Which drink, which add-ons, today's price, is it still in stock

The order itself

When, for pickup where, total, status: received → brewing → ready

The money

Payment amount, method, transaction reference, success or failure

The cart

Where the cart is right now, and its schedule for the day

The app's memory is a database. Today we learn to design that memory — before writing a single line of code. This design is called a data model.
Lecture 3.1

Why Data Modeling Is Essential

  • Cheap now, expensive later — changing a diagram costs minutes; restructuring a live database with real orders costs weeks.
  • The schema outlives the code — apps get rewritten; the data and its structure carry on for years.
  • Structure = speed — a well-modeled database answers "top-selling product this month?" in one query.

Think of a supermarket

  • Aisles = tables
  • Products on shelves = records
  • Price tags = attributes
  • Store signs = keys and indexes

A well-organized store…

  • Customers find things fast
  • Restocking is systematic
  • Nothing is shelved twice
  • New aisles fit the pattern
Key insight: a messy store loses customers; a messy schema loses developers — and eventually, data.

A "thing" the business must remember. This whole table is the entity Books.

One fact about the entity: Title, Price, Stock. Each has a data type.

One concrete instance: the book 1984, priced 12.99, 50 in stock.

Why not use Title as the key? Two books can share a title, and titles get retyped with typos. Keys must be unique and stable — that's why we invent an ID.

Lecture 3.1 · Relationships

One-to-Many (1:N) — the Workhorse

How it's built: the "many" side carries the foreign key. Orders stores CustomerID — the customer's table knows nothing about orders. The fork symbol (crow's foot) always sits on the "many" end.
Lecture 3.1 · Relationships

Many-to-Many (M:N) — the Troublemaker

An order contains many books; a book appears in many orders. Where does the foreign key go?

The fix has a name: a bridge table (also called junction or associative table). Next slide.
Lecture 3.1 · Relationships

The Bridge Table: One M:N → Two 1:N

Without a bridge (wrong)

Orders: OrderID: 1, BookIDs: "1,2,3"

  • Can't query or count individual books
  • Nowhere to put quantity per book
  • Foreign keys can't be enforced

With OrderBooks (right)

1, 1, qty 2 · 1, 2, qty 2 · 1, 3, qty 1

  • One row per order-book pair
  • Quantity and UnitPrice live on the pair
  • Both links are simple, enforceable 1:N
Rule: every M:N in your design becomes a bridge table with two foreign keys — plus any facts that belong to the pair.
Lecture 3.1 · Quick Quiz

Call It: 1:N or M:N?

Shout the answer before it appears. Use the two-question test: "can one A have many Bs — and one B many As?"

Customer ↔ Review

1 : N A customer writes many reviews; each review has one author. FK CustomerID on Reviews.

Student ↔ Course

M : N Bridge table Enrollments — which naturally carries the pair's own fact: the grade.

Order ↔ Invoice

1 : 1 The rare third type. Often merged into one table — kept separate when one half is sensitive or huge.

Product ↔ Category

It depends! One category per product → 1:N. Products in several categories → M:N + bridge. The business rule decides.

The lesson in that last one: cardinality is not a math fact — it encodes your business rules. Two stores can model the same pair differently, and both can be right.
Lecture 3.1

Normalization: Every Fact Lives in Exactly One Place

Redundant (before)

Order 1 · John · john@mail.com
Order 2 · John · john@mail.com
Order 3 · John · jhon@mail.com ← typo

  • John's email is stored 3 times
  • He updates it → you must fix every row
  • Miss one → two "truths" in the database

Normalized (after)

Orders: 1 · CustomerID 101
Orders: 2 · CustomerID 101
Customers: 101 · John · john@mail.com

  • The email exists once, in Customers
  • Update it once — every order "sees" it
  • Orders just point with a foreign key
The smell test: if the same fact is typed into more than one row, your design has a normalization problem. Extract it into its own table and point at it.
It's a trade-off, not a religion: more tables = more joins. For this course, normalize customer data, product data, and anything users can edit. (You'll meet deliberate denormalization when you study performance.)
Lecture 3.1 · Design Review

Find the Flaws: a Table Someone Actually Proposed

Table Orders {
  OrderID          int
  CustomerName     varchar
  CustomerEmail    varchar
  CustomerPassword varchar
  BookTitles       varchar
                   // "1984, Dune, …"
  Total            float
}

Flaw 1 · No primary key

OrderID isn't declared PK — nothing stops two orders with the same ID.

Flaw 2 · Duplicated facts

Customer name and email retyped on every order → the John problem. Extract to Customers + FK.

Flaw 3 · The comma-list

BookTitles is a hidden M:N crying for a bridge — storing fragile titles instead of stable IDs.

Flaw 4 · float + password?!

float rounds pennies away — money is decimal. And a password doesn't belong on Orders at all — hashed, in Users, only.

Score yourself: each flaw takes 30 seconds to fix on a diagram today — and a migration project to fix in production next year. That asymmetry is the whole argument for this lecture.
Lecture 3.1

Schema Best Practices

1 · Every table gets a primary key

Usually an auto-incrementing integer ID. Uniqueness on business fields (Email, ISBN) is added as a separate unique constraint.

2 · Foreign keys, enforced

Declare them as constraints so the database itself refuses an order pointing at a customer who doesn't exist. Integrity by law, not by hope.

3 · Index what you search

Email lookups at login, product searches by title, orders by date — indexes make these fast. (Primary keys are indexed automatically.)

4 · Types and defaults that defend you

DECIMAL for money (never FLOAT), NOT NULL where a value must exist, defaults like Status = 'Pending' and CreatedAt = now.

Naming convention (ours for this course): tables plural (Books), keys as TableNameID, no spaces, consistent casing. Boring names are a feature.

Lecture 3.2

The 5-Step Method

  • Step 1 · Identify entities — hunt the nouns in the business description.
  • Step 2 · Define attributes — what must we remember about each? Pick keys and types.
  • Step 3 · Establish relationships — 1:N or M:N? Bridges where needed.
  • Step 4 · Normalize — hunt repeated facts and extract them.
  • Step 5 · Draw it for real — dbdiagram.io, then export SQL.
Why a bookstore? It's the classic teaching domain: rich enough to need bridges (books have multiple authors!), small enough to finish in one session — and it's the sample project in our course repository.
Lecture 3.2 · Step 1

Identify Entities: Hunt the Nouns

"An online bookstore sells books, each written by one or more authors. Customers create accounts and place orders containing one or more books. Every order is settled by a payment."

Our five entities

Books · Authors · Customers · Orders · Payments

(Two bridge tables will appear in Step 3 — we don't know that yet.)

Selection tips

  • Entities are nouns with independent existence
  • You must need to store facts about it
  • "Account" here is not separate — it's part of Customer
Not every noun survives: "bookstore" is the business itself, not data. "Stock" is an attribute of Books, not an entity. When in doubt ask: would it get its own rows?
Lecture 3.2 · Step 2

Define Attributes — and Choose Keys

Books

Title nvarchar(255), not null

Authors

Choosing wisely

  • PK: invented ID, auto-increment
  • ISBN: unique, but not the PK — it's long, and typos happen
  • decimal(10,2) for money — never float
  • NULL policy: what's truly optional? Bio yes, Title no

Lecture 3.2 · Step 3

Establish Relationships

PairTypeReasoning
Customers → Orders1 : NA customer places many orders; each order has one customer. FK on Orders.
Orders → Payments1 : NAn order can have several payment attempts (failed, retried). FK on Payments.
Orders ↔ BooksM : NOrders hold many books, books appear in many orders → bridge OrderBooks (+ Quantity, UnitPrice).
Books ↔ AuthorsM : NCo-authored books, prolific authors → bridge Author_Book (+ isMain flag).
The decision procedure: for any pair A–B ask two questions. "Can one A have many Bs?" and "Can one B have many As?" One yes = 1:N (FK on the many side). Two yeses = M:N (bridge). Two nos = maybe they're the same table.
Notice: our five entities became seven tables. Bridges always reveal themselves in this step — that's normal, not a mistake.
Lecture 3.2 · Step 4

Normalization Check: the Shipping Address

A classic trap — someone proposes storing the delivery address on every order:

Before

Orders: OrderID 1, CustomerID 101,
Street "123 Elm St", City "New York"…
Orders: OrderID 2, CustomerID 101,
Street "123 Elm St", City "New York"…

Customer moves → their address is wrong on every historical order… or do we want history?

After

Addresses: AddressID 501, CustomerID 101,
Street, City, Country
Orders: OrderID 1, ShippingAddressID 501

Addresses live once; orders point at them. A customer can keep several (home, office).

The subtle part — worth a debate: should editing an address rewrite where old orders were shipped? If not, orders should point at an address row that is never edited, only replaced. Data modeling is full of business questions wearing technical clothes.

Lecture 3.2 · Design Details

Four Details That Separate Pros from Beginners

1 · UnitPrice lives on OrderBooks

Books.Price is today's price. The bridge stores the price at the moment of sale — so raising a price tomorrow doesn't silently rewrite last month's revenue. History must be immutable.

2 · isMain on Author_Book

Bridge tables aren't just glue — they can carry facts about the pair. "Tales of the Unknown" has three authors; isMain marks who goes first on the cover.

3 · Unique ≠ primary key

Email and ISBN get UNIQUE constraints, but the PK stays a plain integer. Business identifiers change (people retype emails); primary keys never should.

4 · Defaults & checks as guardrails

Status defaults to 'Pending', CreatedAt to now, and a CHECK enforces Quantity > 0. The schema quietly refuses bad data even when the app has a bug.

The common thread: the database defends itself. Assume every app that touches it will someday have a bug — because it will.
