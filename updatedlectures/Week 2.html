Lecture 2.1

Why a Canvas, Not a 40-Page Plan?

  • Strategy changes fast — a long business plan is outdated before it's printed. A canvas is redrawn in minutes.
  • Shared language — nine boxes everyone understands: teammates, investors, professors. No jargon required.
  • Assumptions become visible — every sticky note is a guess you can point at, argue about, and test.
  • Built to iterate — move a note, the model changes. Try doing that with chapter 4 of a business plan.
🧠 Key idea: the canvas doesn't replace thinking — it exposes it. A weak model has nowhere to hide on one page.
Lecture 2.1 · Our Running Example

Campus Coffee Cart ☕ + 📱

A specialty coffee cart that moves around campus following the crowd — paired with an app where you preorder and pay, then grab your cup with zero queue.

🛒 The cart

  • Espresso machine, grinder, small footprint
  • Moves to where students are: quad at 8–11am, library at 1–3pm
  • Walk-up orders still welcome

📱 The app

  • Live cart location — "where's the cart right now?"
  • Preorder & pay — order from class, pick up on the walk
  • Loyalty — digital punch card, buy 9 get 1 free

🧪 Assumptions we'll have to defend today

  • Students value speed + quality over the cheaper cafeteria coffee
  • People will install an app for a coffee cart
  • The right spots/times exist — and campus will let us use them
🎯 By the end of this lecture, every one of these assumptions will live in a specific box of the canvas — where we can attack it.
Lecture 2.1 · The Map

The Canvas at a Glance: 9 Blocks, 3 Zones

→ Right side: the market

Desirability — who wants this and why? Segments, value, channels, relationships.

← Left side: the operation

Feasibility — can we actually deliver it? Resources, activities, partners.

↓ Bottom: the money

Viability — does revenue beat cost? If not, it's a hobby, not a business.

💎 Value Propositions sit in the center on purpose: it's the bridge. Everything on the left exists to produce it; everything on the right exists to deliver and monetize it.
Lecture 2.1 · The Logic

How the Blocks Play Together

  • 💎↔👥 Value ↔ Segments is the heart: a promise that a specific group actually wants. Get this fit wrong and the other 7 boxes don't matter.
  • 📣❤️ Channels & Relationships carry the value to the segment — how they discover you, buy, and come back.
  • 💰 Revenue is the segment saying "yes" with money — pricing only makes sense relative to their alternatives.
  • 🧰⚙️🤝 Resources, Activities, Partners are what it takes to keep the promise, day after day.
  • 📉 Costs are the price of the left side. Viability = Revenue − Cost.
✅ Coherence test: pick any sticky note and ask "which other boxes does this one need?" If the answer is "none," it probably doesn't belong on the canvas.
Lecture 2.1 · Block 1 of 9

👥 Customer Segments

📖 Definition: the specific groups of people you create value for. Not "everyone" — specific.

❓ Guiding questions

  • Who has the problem? Who pays? Who uses?
  • Who are the early adopters — the impatient ones who'll try you first?

☕ Coffee Cart answers

  • Primary: undergrads rushing between classes (8–11am peak)
  • Secondary: faculty & staff with predictable breaks
  • Early adopters: app-first students near the main quad — they already preorder everything else

🔗 Plays with

Value Propositions — each segment hears a different promise: students want speed, staff want quality & a walk. The app tells us who's who.

⚠️ Pitfall #1 of all canvases: "our customer is everyone on campus." If everyone is your customer, no one is your early adopter. Start narrow, expand later.
Lecture 2.1 · Block 2 of 9

💎 Value Propositions

📖 Definition: the bundle of benefits you promise a segment — pains removed, gains created. Benefits, never features.

❓ Guiding questions

  • What job is the customer trying to get done? (Here: caffeine + make it to class on time)
  • Why you and not the alternatives? (cafeteria, vending machine, off-campus café)

☕ Coffee Cart answers

  • ⏱️ Skip the line — preorder in app, grab & go in seconds
  • 📍 The cart finds you — live location; it's parked on your route, not across campus
  • ☕ Café quality — real espresso vs vending-machine sludge
  • 🎁 Pay & earn in app — no cash fumbling, punch card fills itself

🔗 Plays with

Segments (the fit!) — and notice: two of our four benefits only exist because of the app. Remove the app and the value proposition halves.

📝 The one-sentence test: "For students rushing between classes, who want good coffee without being late, we provide preordered espresso from a cart on their route — unlike the cafeteria queue or the 15-minute walk to a café."
Lecture 2.1 · Block 3 of 9

📣 Channels

📖 Definition: how customers discover you, buy from you, and receive the value — the full journey, not just "marketing."

🗺️ The 5 channel stages — Coffee Cart

  • 1 · Awareness: Instagram reels, campus posters, student-club shoutouts
  • 2 · Consideration: app-store page, friends showing the live map
  • 3 · Purchase: 📱 app preorder + pay, or classic walk-up
  • 4 · Delivery: the cart itself, parked on schedule; push "your order is ready"
  • 5 · After-sales: in-app feedback, loyalty nudges ("1 punch from free!")

🔗 Plays with

Value Prop — "skip the line" is only deliverable because the purchase channel (app) and delivery channel (cart) are connected in real time.

⚠️ Defend it: "What if people don't download the app?" — walk-up still works day one; the app earns its install with a free first coffee. Channel redundancy is a feature, not indecision.
Lecture 2.1 · Block 4 of 9

❤️ Customer Relationships

📖 Definition: the type of relationship each segment expects — personal, automated, self-service, community, co-creation.

☕ Coffee Cart answers

  • 🤝 Personal: baristas know regulars by name and "the usual"
  • 🤖 Automated: push notifications — "cart is 2 min from you", "order ready"
  • 🗳️ Community: students vote next month's seasonal flavor in the app
  • 🎁 Loyalty: digital punch card — buy 9, get 1 free, automatically

💡 Why it matters

Relationships drive retention — and Week 1 taught us retention is where LTV comes from. A personal hello costs $0 and no competitor's app can copy it.

🔗 Plays with

Segments & Revenue — regulars who feel known churn less and prepay more (wait for the Cart Card two slides ahead).

Lecture 2.1 · Block 5 of 9

💰 Revenue Streams

📖 Definition: how each segment says "yes" with money — and why the price makes sense against their alternatives.

☕ The menu (unit sales)

  • Espresso $2.00 · Americano $2.50 · Latte $3.50 · Cold brew $3.75
  • Add-ons: oat milk +$0.50 · flavor shot +$0.30

📱 App-powered streams

  • Morning Pass: $12/week = 1 coffee/day Mon–Fri (a subscription!)
  • Cart Card: prepay $25, get $27.50 credit — cash up front, loyalty locked in

🔗 Plays with

Value Prop — we charge more than the cafeteria ($1.50) because we sell time + quality, and less than the café ($4.50+) because we have no rent.

💡 Notice the Week 1 patterns: unit sales, add-ons (AOV ↑), a subscription (recurring revenue), and prepayment (negative churn pressure) — all in one tiny cart.
Lecture 2.1 · Block 6 of 9

🧰 Key Resources

📖 Definition: the assets without which you cannot deliver the value proposition. Test each one: "no this → no business?"

☕ Coffee Cart answers

  • 🏗️ Physical: the cart, espresso machine, grinder, water & power setup
  • 🧑‍🍳 Human: trained baristas (speed and friendliness are the product)
  • 📱 Digital: the app — ordering, payments, live location, loyalty data
  • 💵 Financial: startup float for beans, milk, cups
  • 🏷️ Intellectual: the brand students recognize & recipes

🔗 Plays with

Value Prop & Activities — the app is now officially a key resource: it powers 2 of our 4 value points and 2 of our 4 revenue streams.

⚠️ Defend it: "Do students really need a custom app — isn't that expensive?" Hold that thought for Key Partnerships: we won't build it from scratch.
Lecture 2.1 · Block 7 of 9

⚙️ Key Activities

📖 Definition: the 3–6 things you must do excellently, every day. Everything else is noise.

☕ Coffee Cart answers

  • 🗺️ Route planning & daily setup — being in the right spot is the business
  • ☕ Rush-hour brewing & service — 90 seconds per order or the "skip the line" promise dies
  • 📦 Sourcing — beans, milk, cups, every week, no stockouts
  • 📱 App operations — update location, menu, push notifications, watch preorder queue
  • 📸 Social & loyalty — posts, flavor votes, rewards

🔗 Plays with

Resources (activities use resources) and Value Prop — every activity above maps to a promise on the right side. If one doesn't, cut it.

🎯 Quick check: "designing a beautiful logo" — key activity or noise? What about "restocking oat milk"? Defend your answer with the canvas.
Lecture 2.1 · Block 8 of 9

🤝 Key Partnerships

📖 Definition: who helps you deliver — reducing cost, risk, or both. Ask: "buy vs build?" and "who already has what we need?"

☕ Coffee Cart answers

  • 🫘 Local roaster — quality beans at wholesale, their brand on our menu
  • 🏛️ Campus administration — permits & prime locations (the existential partner)
  • 💳 Payment provider — in-app payments; we never touch card numbers (remember SSL week?)
  • 📱 App platform — white-label ordering app (Shopify-style), not custom code
  • 🔧 Equipment service — a dead espresso machine = zero revenue days

🔗 Plays with

Resources & Costs — the app answer: a white-label platform turns a $20k build into ~$50/month. Partnership just defended our feasibility.

⚠️ Defend it: "What if campus revokes your spot?" — that's why admin is a partner, not an obstacle: offer a small revenue share and become the campus's success story, not its problem.
Lecture 2.1 · Block 9 of 9

📉 Cost Structure

📖 Definition: every significant cost of running the left side of the canvas. Split them: fixed (pay regardless) vs variable (per cup).

☕ Coffee Cart answers

  • 🏗️ One-time (capex): cart + machine ≈ $5,000–7,000
  • 📌 Fixed monthly: permit, app platform fee (~$50), storage, maintenance plan
  • 📈 Variable per cup: beans, milk, cup & lid, barista hours, ~2.9% payment fee

🧭 Trace every cost

Each cost should point back at a Resource, Activity, or Partner. A cost with no home upstairs is money leaking out of the model.

🔗 Plays with

Revenue — the whole bottom row is one equation: Revenue − Cost = the reason to exist. Let's compute it on the next slides.

Lecture 2.1 · Defending the Money

Does the Bottom Row Work? Unit Economics

One latte, dissected — then one day, then one month:

LineMathResult
☕ Price of a latteset by value vs alternatives, not by cost$3.50
📉 Variable cost per cupbeans $0.45 + milk $0.35 + cup $0.25 + pay fee $0.10−$1.15
💚 Margin per cup$3.50 − $1.15 = $2.3567% ✅
📅 A good day120 cups × ~$2.20 avg margin≈ $264/day
📌 Fixed costs per daypermit + app + storage + maintenance ÷ days≈ −$60/day
🏆 Operating profit≈ $204/day × 20 campus days≈ $4,000/mo
🧮 And the capex? $6,000 cart ÷ $4,000/month ≈ paid back in ~6 weeks of good days. Now the class debate: what does a bad day (rain, exams, summer) do to this table? Which block absorbs the shock?
Lecture 2.1 · Defend the Model

Attack the Cart — the Canvas Answers

Every objection lands in a specific block. That's what "the blocks play together" means in practice:

🗡️ "The cafeteria is half your price."

💎 VP + 👥 CS answer: we don't sell to price-shoppers. Our segment pays $2 extra to save 10 minutes and drink real espresso. Different segment, different value.

🗡️ "Nobody installs an app for coffee."

📣 CH + ❤️ CR answer: walk-up works day one; the app is earned with a free first coffee, then keeps them via preorder + punch card. Install is the reward, not the barrier.

🗡️ "Campus can kick you out tomorrow."

🤝 KP answer: admin is a partner with revenue share and a success story, not a landlord to dodge. Biggest risk → strongest partnership.

🗡️ "A cart can't make real money."

📉💰 Bottom-row answer: 67% margin, ~$4k/month operating profit, capex back in 6 weeks — with numbers, not vibes.

🛡️ The lesson: a defended model isn't one with no weaknesses — it's one where every attack has an addressed block. If an objection has no answering block, your canvas has a hole.
Lecture 2.1 · Evidence

Find the Riskiest Assumptions — Then Test Cheap

⚠️ Our riskiest guesses

  • Demand actually peaks 8–11am at the quad
  • Students will pay $3.50 for a latte
  • Campus grants the hotspot permits
  • We can serve a preorder in under 90 seconds

🧪 This-week tests (total budget: ~$0)

  • Interviews: 10 students about morning coffee habits
  • Fake preorders: QR poster + Google Form at the quad — count scans
  • Pop-up: 1-day dry run with a borrowed machine
  • Price A/B: $3.25 Tuesday vs $3.75 Thursday
🔬 Rule: riskiest assumption first, cheapest test first. Remember Zappos from Week 1 — they tested demand with someone else's shoes before buying inventory.
📝 For your project: you'll star ★ your two riskiest assumptions on your own canvas and design one test for each — it's in the rubric.
Lecture 2.1 · Before You Build Yours

Common Pitfalls & Pro Tips

🚫 Pitfalls

  • "Everyone is our customer"
  • Features instead of benefits ("we have an app" vs "skip the line")
  • Listing 15 activities — missing the 4 that matter
  • Pricing by cost alone, ignoring the customer's alternatives
  • A canvas that never changes after week 1

✅ Pro tips

  • Start narrow — early adopters first, "everyone" later
  • Sticky notes, not permanent marker: move them as you learn
  • Pair every block with one test you can run this week
  • Read the canvas out loud left-to-right — incoherence is audible
🔁 The canvas is a living document. The Coffee Cart canvas you saw today is draft #1 — a real one gets rewritten five times in a semester. That's the point.

Pitch Essentials

Lecture 2.2

The 6 Must-Have Pitch Slides

Airbnb's legendary 2008 deck raised $600k with essentially these six — your canvas already contains all of them:

1

😖 Problem

A pain someone currently suffers. Canvas source: Segments + their alternatives.

2

💡 Solution

Your promise, in one sentence. Canvas source: Value Proposition.

3

📊 Market

Who + how many + growing? Canvas source: Segments, sized with Week 1 tools.

4

💰 Model

How money flows in. Canvas source: Revenue Streams + unit economics.

5

🧑‍🤝‍🧑 Team

Why you can pull this off. Canvas source: Key Resources (human) + Partners.

6

🙏 Ask

What you need next. For class: your two tests. For investors: the check.

🗺️ See it? A pitch is just the canvas re-ordered into a story: right side → problem & solution, bottom row → model, left side → credibility.
Lecture 2.2 · Worked Example

The Coffee Cart Pitch — in 60 Seconds

SlideWhat we'd say
😖 Problem"Between classes you have 10 minutes. The cafeteria queue is 12. The good café is a 15-minute walk. So you drink bad coffee — or none."
💡 Solution"A specialty coffee cart that moves to where you are — preorder and pay in the app, grab your cup on the walk to class."
📊 Market"9,000 students + 1,100 staff; 60% drink coffee daily; peak demand mapped to 3 campus hotspots."
💰 Model"$3.50 lattes at 67% margin, plus a $12/week Morning Pass — ≈$4k/month operating profit per cart."
🧑‍🤝‍🧑 Team"Two of us are baristas, one built stores on a no-code platform. Roaster and campus admin are onboard."
🙏 Ask"This month: permits for two hotspots and 200 app preorders — before we buy the second cart."
⏱️ Notice: six sentences ≈ 60 seconds. Numbers where it counts, story everywhere else — and every sentence traceable to a canvas block.
Lecture 2.2

Storytelling & Design Tips

  • 🎬 Open with the pain, not your logo — make the room feel the 12-minute queue before you sell the cure.
  • 🔢 One number per slide that the audience should remember — 67% margin beats a wall of spreadsheet.
  • 🖼️ Show, don't list — a photo of the queue, a screenshot of the app's live map. Bullets are for handouts.
  • 🗣️ Rehearse the 60-second version — if you can't pitch it in a minute, you don't own the idea yet.
  • ❓ Plan for the attack — bring the objections slide from today; answering a hard question well is the best pitch move there is.
📚 Study later: Airbnb's and Uber's first decks (both are public) — count how few words they used.
