Lecture 1.1

What is E-Commerce?

  • Definition & scope — buying and selling of goods, services, and information over digital networks, plus everything that supports it: payments, logistics, marketing, data.
  • Why MIS? — e-commerce lives exactly where business models meet technology execution. Someone has to translate "we sell coffee subscriptions" into systems that actually work.
  • Our course journey — Pitch → No-code MVP → Full-stack build (MSSQL + Node.js). By the end you will have shipped a real store, three different ways.
💡 Rule of thumb: if a transaction touches a screen at any point — discovery, order, payment, or delivery tracking — e-commerce thinking applies.

Lecture 1.1 · Era Example — 1990s

1995: Selling Books to Strangers

📚 Amazon's opening move

Jeff Bezos picked books deliberately: millions of titles (no physical store could stock them all), standard products (no need to touch before buying), easy to ship.

Lesson: the first product should fit the channel's strengths, not just your passion.

🛒 The first shopping carts

Before cart software, ordering online meant emailing a list or filling a raw form. The "cart + checkout" pattern we still use was invented in this era — and hasn't fundamentally changed since.

😨 The fear

"Type my credit card into a computer? Never." Surveys in the mid-90s showed security as the #1 reason people refused to buy online.

🔒 The fix

Netscape ships SSL in 1994 (the handshake you just saw). The padlock icon becomes the most valuable UI element in commerce history.

🔁 Same story today: every new channel (social checkout, AI agents buying for you) replays this exact trust curve — fear → security tech → mass adoption.
Lecture 1.1 · Era Example — 2000s

PayPal & the Stranger-Payment Problem

eBay connected millions of buyers and sellers — who had zero reason to trust each other.

The problem

Mail a cheque to a stranger and hope a Beanie Baby arrives? Sellers shipped and got stiffed; buyers paid and got nothing.

The wedge

PayPal sat in the middle: buyer pays PayPal, PayPal pays seller. Add buyer protection, dispute resolution, and fraud detection on top.

The result

Trust became a product feature. eBay bought PayPal for $1.5B in 2002. Every gateway since (Stripe, Checkout.com) sells the same thing: trust as a service.

⚔️ Fun fact: PayPal's early fraud losses nearly killed it — the anti-fraud tooling they built to survive became the industry's playbook (and its alumni later founded Tesla, LinkedIn, YouTube… the "PayPal Mafia").
🧠 For your project: you will never build payments from scratch — you'll integrate a gateway. Knowing why they exist tells you what to check when choosing one: fraud tools, dispute handling, payout speed, fees.
Lecture 1.1 · Era Example — 2010s

The Phone Becomes the Mall

📱 Mobile commerce

Smartphones + 1-click wallets (Apple Pay, Google Pay) collapse checkout from 10 form fields to a thumbprint. Cart abandonment drops when friction drops.

Design consequence: mobile-first layouts, big buttons, guest checkout.

🤳 Social selling

Discovery moves from search ("I need shoes") to feeds ("I didn't know I wanted these shoes"). Influencers become storefronts; Instagram adds in-app checkout.

China leapfrogs everyone: WeChat merges chat + wallet + shop in one app.

🚚 Logistics platforms

Fulfilment by Amazon, Shopify + third-party logistics: a solo founder can now offer 2-day shipping like a giant. Delivery speed becomes a marketing feature.

📊 The numbers flip

By late 2010s, more than half of e-commerce traffic worldwide is mobile. If your store demo isn't tested on a phone, it isn't tested.

Lecture 1.1 · Era Example — 2020s

AI Commerce: Too Much Choice, Solved

🎯 Recommendation & search

~35% of Amazon purchases start from "recommended for you". AI search understands "warm jacket for Beirut winter under $80" — not just keywords.

🕶️ AR try-ons

IKEA Place puts the sofa in your living room; sunglasses try-on cuts returns. Returns are the silent killer of online margins — AR attacks them directly.

🔁 Subscriptions everywhere

Razors, coffee, pet food, software. Predictable recurring revenue is so valuable that one-time-purchase brands re-engineer themselves around it. (More math on this in a few slides.)

⚡ Same-day & q-commerce

Dark stores + route optimization = groceries in 20 minutes. Speed expectations set by the biggest players become the baseline everyone is judged against.

🤔 Discussion: which of these four could a student project realistically use? (Hint: recommendation via simple "customers also bought" queries is absolutely in reach of your MSSQL phase.)
Lecture 1.1

Business Models

  • Who sells to whom: B2C, B2B, C2C, D2C — and hybrids that mix them.
  • How money recurs: one-time sales vs subscriptions & memberships (recurring revenue).
  • Marketplaces: you sell access, not products — network effects, take rates, compliance.
  • How we keep score: CAC, LTV, AOV, conversion rate, churn.
🧭 Why this matters now: your Phase-1 pitch must name its model and its metrics. "We'll sell stuff online" is not a business model.
Lecture 1.1 · Examples

Model Gallery: Who Sells to Whom

B2C

Business → Consumer

Amazon retail, Zara.com, noon

High volume, low order value, marketing-driven. Wins on convenience & price.

B2B

Business → Business

Alibaba, Amazon Business

Fewer, bigger orders. Quotes, credit terms, approval workflows. Boring = profitable.

C2C

Consumer → Consumer

eBay, Facebook Marketplace, Vinted

Platform owns trust & payments; users own inventory. Zero stock risk.

D2C

Direct → Consumer

Warby Parker, Dollar Shave Club

Brand skips retailers, owns the customer relationship & the data.

💈 Mini-case — Dollar Shave Club: a $4,500 YouTube video + subscription razors vs Gillette's retail empire. Acquired by Unilever for $1B five years later. The moat wasn't razors — it was the direct customer relationship + recurring revenue.
🎯 Quick check: a university student selling thrifted clothes via Instagram DMs — which model is that? What would change if she moved to a marketplace app?
Lecture 1.1 · Examples

Subscriptions: Why Investors Love Recurring Revenue

🛍️ One-time model

Sell 200 orders → $7,000

Next month starts at $0. Every sale must be re-won with new marketing spend.

🔁 Subscription model

200 subscribers × $35 → $7,000/mo

Next month starts at ~$6,650 (assuming 5% churn). Marketing adds on top of a base that compounds.

ExampleWhat recursThe hook
Amazon PrimeMembership feeFree shipping makes every other purchase easier
NetflixAccessCancel-anytime lowers the barrier to start
HelloFresh / coffee clubsPhysical goodsRemoves a recurring chore (deciding + shopping)
⚠️ The catch — churn: subscriptions leak. At 5% monthly churn you lose half your base in ~13 months. Retention (not acquisition) is where subscription businesses live or die.

🐔🥚 The cold-start problem: the flywheel is brutal to start — no buyers without sellers, no sellers without buyers. Classic fix: fake one side first (Airbnb seeded listings by hand; marketplaces subsidize early sellers).
Lecture 1.1

How We Keep Score: The 5 Core Metrics

CAC

Customer Acquisition Cost

Marketing spend ÷ new customers won. "What does one new customer cost me?"

LTV

Lifetime Value

Profit a customer generates over their whole relationship with you.

AOV

Average Order Value

Revenue ÷ number of orders. Raised by bundles, upsells, free-shipping thresholds.

Conversion rate

Orders ÷ visitors. E-commerce typically lands around 1–3% — yes, 97+ of every 100 visitors leave without buying.

Churn

% of customers/subscribers lost per period. The gravity that recurring-revenue businesses fight forever.

🏆 The one ratio to remember: LTV : CAC ≥ 3 is the classic health bar. Below 3, you're buying customers for more than they're worth. Let's compute it on a real example →
Lecture 1.1 · Worked Example

"Beirut Beans" — One Month, All 5 Metrics

A small online coffee store. Follow the numbers as they build:

StepCalculationResult
🧲 Traffic$2,000 ads bring 10,000 visitors10,000 visits
🛒 Conversion200 orders ÷ 10,000 visitors2% ✅ (normal)
💵 AOV$7,000 revenue ÷ 200 orders$35
🧮 CAC$2,000 ÷ 100 new customers (rest were returning)$20

Market Research

Lecture 1.2

Market Research: Why It Matters

  • De-risk with data before building — the #1 reason startups fail is "no market need", not bad code.
  • Quantify demand — how many people search for this? Is it growing or fading? Seasonal or steady?
  • Find underserved segments — the gap is rarely "no one sells X"; it's "no one sells X for this group, this way".
  • Translate findings into a value proposition — research isn't a report, it's the raw material of your pitch.
💸 The economics: a week of research costs you $0 and some Google time. Building the wrong product costs months. This is the cheapest insurance you will ever buy.
Lecture 1.2 · Example

Tale of Two Launches

❌ Juicero (2016)

  • Raised $120M to build a $699 Wi-Fi juice press
  • Engineering-first: 4 years, custom hardware, an app
  • Journalists discovered you could squeeze the juice packs by hand
  • Dead within 16 months of launch

Nobody asked: "would people pay for this?"

✅ Zappos (1999)

  • Question: "will people buy shoes online without trying them?"
  • Test: photograph shoes at local stores, list them online
  • When an order came in, founder bought the pair at retail and shipped it — losing money per sale, on purpose
  • Demand proven → then build inventory & systems → sold to Amazon for $1.2B
🧪 The pattern: Zappos spent days to learn what Juicero never learned in 4 years. Your Phase-2 no-code MVP is exactly this kind of test — demand first, engineering second.
Lecture 1.2

Core Tools & Signals

📈 Google Trends

Signal: demand direction & seasonality. Free. Compare terms, filter by country, spot rising queries.

🌍 Similarweb

Signal: competitor traffic — volume, sources (search? social? ads?), top countries, engagement.

📊 Statista / Think with Google

Signal: market size & consumer behavior reports — the numbers that make your pitch credible.

👂 Social listening

Signal: pain points in the wild — Reddit threads, TikTok comments, Instagram DMs. Complaints = opportunities.

🧭 Rule: one tool is an anecdote, two tools agreeing is a signal, three is a thesis. Never pitch on a single data source.

Lecture 1.2 · Example

A 10-Minute Competitor Audit (Similarweb)

Mock data — two skincare shops that look identical from the outside:

🧴 GlowShop.com

1.2M visits/mo

  • 🔎 62% from paid ads
  • ⏱️ 1.1 min avg visit · 71% bounce
  • 🌍 Top geo: US, UK

Read: buying traffic. Stop the ads, the business stops. Vulnerable.

🌿 DermaDaily.com

300K visits/mo

  • 🔎 58% organic search + 22% direct
  • ⏱️ 4.3 min avg visit · 38% bounce
  • 🌍 Top geo: US, Canada, UAE

Read: loyal audience that searches for them by name. Durable.

🕵️ The insight a beginner misses: the smaller site is the stronger business. Traffic quality beats traffic quantity — and traffic sources tell you which fights you can actually win as a newcomer (hint: don't out-bid GlowShop on ads).
🎯 For your audit: capture for each competitor — monthly visits, top 3 traffic sources, top geos, and one thing their customers complain about (check reviews).
Lecture 1.2 · Hands-On

Activity: Find a Market Gap

  • 1 · Pick a category — fashion, food, tech, or services. Pick one you actually know.
  • 2 · Google Trends — compare your top 3 terms; note direction & seasonality.
  • 3 · Audit 2 competitors — Similarweb: traffic sources & geos (like the last slide).
  • 4 · Mine 3 pain points — reviews, Reddit, TikTok comments. Real quotes, not guesses.
  • 5 · Write 1 hypothesis — and prep a 2-minute pitch for next class.

📝 Hypothesis template

"[Segment] struggles with [pain point] when buying [category]. We believe a store offering [your angle] would win them, because [evidence from ≥2 tools]."

A hypothesis you can't test is an opinion. Make every blank specific.

⏰ Grading the pitch: 2 minutes, max 3 visuals — one Trends screenshot, one competitor fact, one customer quote. Evidence beats enthusiasm.
